# Mental Models

Mental models are interpretive lenses. Use them to reframe a situation before deciding.

Different from principles (decision rules to follow) and anti-patterns (behaviors to stop). A model does not tell the user what to do — it tells them what they are looking at.

## Worth vs. Busy

Activity is not progress. Work is worth doing only when it has a plausible path to revenue, retention, distribution, learning, margin, speed, trust, or leverage.

When a user lists their week, separate motion from impact before scoring or scheduling. The list of "things I did" is not the same as the list of "things that mattered."

## Evidence Ladder

Every claim about demand sits on a rung. Use lower rungs to design tests; use higher rungs to justify commitment.

1. Opinion
2. Compliment
3. Click
4. Reply or signup
5. Qualified conversation
6. Referral
7. Deposit, payment, signed pilot, or signed contract
8. Repeat purchase, retained usage, renewal, or margin improvement

A buyer who pays today is more reliable than a hundred who say they would pay someday. When in doubt, demand a higher rung before spending more.

## The Buyer Clock

A problem becomes valuable the moment a buyer feels it. Timing can matter as much as severity.

Prioritize moments with deadlines, new budgets, broken workflows, compliance changes, launches, churn risk, new hires, growth targets, or visible opportunity cost. Cold problems pay nothing; hot problems pay quickly.

## Time as Investment Capital

Treat time like a portfolio. Allocate most to proven winners, some to promising tests, and little to maintenance or speculative noise.

If a recurring task would not deserve cash funding, question whether it deserves prime attention. The user's calendar is a balance sheet.

## The Sharp Ask

Vague asks create vague answers. Sharp asks create evidence.

Replace "Would this be interesting?" with "Will you book a call by Friday?", "Will you pay a deposit this week?", "Will you introduce me to two buyers by next Tuesday?", or "Will you try this workflow for seven days?" Specificity converts conversation into commitment.

## Friction Reveals Design

Where buyers hesitate, users churn, teams stall, or delivery breaks, the business is showing the next improvement opportunity.

Do not hide friction. Name it, measure it, and decide whether it points to offer, channel, product, price, trust, or execution. Smooth-looking processes that hide friction are more dangerous than ugly processes that surface it.

## Distribution Tax

Every idea pays a distribution tax. If the user cannot reach the buyer repeatedly and credibly, the idea is more expensive than it looks.

Ask about channel before recommending more build. The cost of being unknown is rarely on the spreadsheet but always on the timeline.

## Bottleneck Before Breadth

Adding more projects rarely fixes a blocked system. Find the current constraint — demand, conversion, delivery, retention, margin, talent, cash, or attention — and move it before expanding the surface area.

A wider boat does not move faster if the same paddle is broken.

## Repeatability Test

One win is useful. A repeatable win is a business asset.

For any positive result, ask what must be true for it to happen again with less luck, less effort, better margin, or better quality. If nothing makes it repeatable, count it as a learning, not a strategy.

## Promise vs. Process

Buyers care about result, risk reduction, speed, status, ease, or confidence. Process matters only when it increases belief or reduces risk.

Lead with the promise. Explain the machinery only when belief needs reinforcement. The user's craft pride often makes them invert this order, to their cost.

## Constraint as Strategy

Limited time, money, team, or attention forces better choices. Use constraints to shrink tests, sharpen buyers, and remove polish.

A constraint should make the next action smaller, not the ambition foggier. "I have less" is a sharper input than "I want more."

## The Drain Test

Some work is not just low value; it drains the energy needed for high-value work.

If a recurring task creates little value and heavy drag, remove, simplify, delegate, batch, or convert it into a single decision. Drain compounds invisibly.

## Optionality vs. Commitment

Keeping every option open blocks progress. Use small tests to earn commitment.

Stay flexible about tactics. Be decisive about the current scoreboard, the named buyer, and the proof threshold. Optionality without commitment is procrastination wearing a suit.

## Asset Over Effort

Favor work that leaves behind a reusable asset — insight, distribution, process, content, code, templates, relationships, data, or trained operators.

Avoid work that disappears the moment effort stops, unless it produces immediate cash or critical learning.

## Buyer-Problem-Path

A real opportunity has three pieces aligned: a named buyer, a painful active problem, and a credible path the user can reach the buyer with.

If any piece is missing, the opportunity is theoretical. Tests should target the weakest piece first, not the most exciting one.

## Cost of Inaction

Most buyer decisions hinge less on the offer's appeal and more on the cost of doing nothing.

Quantify the buyer's status quo: lost revenue, wasted hours, missed timing, compliance risk, churn, or opportunity foregone. Sell against the status quo, not the competition.

## Concentration Beats Coverage

Twenty deliberate reps in one channel teach more than five reps each in four channels.

Choose one channel and one offer per season. Concentration produces signal; coverage produces noise. Variance hides under the average.

## Trust Stack

Buyers grant trust in stages: presence (do they know you exist) → credibility (do they believe you can do it) → safety (do they believe it will not hurt to try) → preference (do they choose you over alternatives).

Diagnose where in the stack the buyer is stuck before changing the offer. A safety problem cannot be solved with credibility content; a credibility problem cannot be solved with reach.

## Winners Concentrate, Losers Diversify

In any phase, a small number of bets, channels, customers, or offers produce most of the value.

When wins start to repeat, double down before diversifying. Premature diversification is the most common path back to mediocrity.

## Reversibility Test

Decisions vary by how easy they are to undo. Reversible choices deserve speed; irreversible choices deserve care.

For any pending decision, ask: "If this is wrong, how much does it cost to reverse?" Speed up the cheap ones; slow down the expensive ones.

## Latent Demand vs. Created Demand

Some markets have buyers actively searching for a solution; others require the user to teach the buyer the problem exists.

Latent demand is faster, cheaper, and easier to test. Created demand is more defensible but requires patience and content compounding. Choose deliberately, not by default.
