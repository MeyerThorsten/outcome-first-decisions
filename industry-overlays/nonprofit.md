# Industry Overlay: Nonprofit

Load when the user runs a mission-driven organization funded by donors, grants, members, or program fees — charities, foundations, associations, social enterprises, community organizations.

Apply on top of the parent skill's defaults. The "buyer" is whoever funds the mission — donor, grantmaker, member, or fee-paying program participant. The Buyer Evidence Ladder applies to funders exactly as it applies to customers.

## Unit of Value

**Unrestricted funding per program outcome delivered, weighted by funder retention.**

Not total raised, not grant count, not social reach. Restricted grants that fund only the program they audit leave the organization structurally broke. Value lives in funders who renew, money the organization can allocate, and outcomes that can be shown rather than narrated.

## Most-Believed-But-Wrong Narrative

**"We need more awareness — if more people knew about the cause, funding would follow."**

Almost always false. Awareness without an ask converts to nothing. The real problem is usually one of:

- **No repeatable ask.** The organization has stories but no specific, costed, deadline-bound funding offer.
- **Funder concentration.** One grant or donor is most of the budget, and renewal anxiety drives all decisions.
- **Outcome vagueness.** Impact is described in adjectives, so renewals depend on relationships instead of results.
- **Program sprawl.** New initiatives chase each funder's interests until nothing is delivered excellently.

Sharpen the ask and the outcome evidence before any awareness spend.

## Buyer-Behavior Signals (rung 6+)

1. **Unrestricted or multi-year commitment.** A funder gives unrestricted money or signs a multi-year pledge — the funding equivalent of a prepaid contract.
2. **Renewal at equal or higher level.** A donor or grantmaker renews without being chased, or increases their gift after seeing the outcome report.
3. **Funder-initiated introduction.** A current funder introduces the organization to another funder by name — the referral rung, and in philanthropy the strongest one.

## Hardest-to-Fake Proof Test

**A costed, capped, deadline-bound offer put to 20 warm funders in 14 days.**

Package one program outcome as a specific offer: "$X funds Y outcome for Z people by [date]; we are raising exactly $N from 10 funders by [deadline]." Put it directly to the 20 warmest contacts with a real ask and a real deadline. If 5+ commit, the case for support works — systematize it. If 0–1 commit while everyone praises the mission, the organization has sympathy, not funders, and the offer needs rebuilding.

## Common Nonprofit Anti-Patterns to Watch

- **Grant-chasing drift.** Reshaping programs around every RFP until mission and competence diverge.
- **Gala economics.** Events that net 20% of gross after staff time, counted as fundraising success.
- **Awareness campaigns without asks.** Reach metrics presented to the board as progress toward funding.
- **Free-program reflex.** Refusing earned-revenue or fee-based models that funders would respect and that reduce dependency.
- **Impact theater.** Reporting activities (workshops held, flyers distributed) instead of outcomes (people housed, scores raised).

## Scoreboard Defaults for Nonprofit Users

If the user cannot pick one number, default to one of:

- Unrestricted funding raised per quarter (cash, not pledges).
- Funder retention rate (% of last year's funders renewing at equal or higher level).
- Cost per outcome delivered (fully loaded program cost ÷ verified outcomes).

Pick whichever is currently the bottleneck per Bottleneck Before Breadth.
