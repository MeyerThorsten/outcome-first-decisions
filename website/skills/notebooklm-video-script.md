## Outcome-First Decisions: A Skill That Won't Motivate You

Most AI tools sold to founders are built to make you feel good. They generate plans, draft strategies, produce roadmaps, and tell you that your idea has potential. They are productivity theater dressed as productivity. They make a busy person feel busier in a more impressive way.

Outcome-First Decisions is the opposite. It is an AI agent skill that turns business uncertainty into proof, decisions, and action — and refuses to do anything else. It does not motivate. It does not cheerlead. It asks for buyer evidence, picks one scoreboard number, and forces a written kill criterion before more time gets spent.

The premise is simple: if a decision lacks a named buyer, a measurable scoreboard number, a 7-day proof test, and a kill criterion, the skill refuses to commit to a verdict. It asks the smallest question that fills the gap. The job is not to make every idea sound promising. The job is to help the user spend scarce attention on work that creates measurable business value, and to remove work that only feels productive.

## The Buyer Evidence Ladder

The skill is built around one central concept: the Buyer Evidence Ladder. Every claim about demand sits on a rung. There are eight rungs.

Rung one is opinion. Someone said they like the idea.

Rung two is compliment. Someone praised the concept without action.

Rung three is click. Someone clicked an ad or a link.

Rung four is reply or signup. Someone responded to outreach or joined a list.

Rung five is qualified conversation. Someone with budget and authority discussed their problem in detail.

Rung six is referral. A buyer introduced you to another named buyer.

Rung seven is deposit, payment, signed pilot, or signed contract. Money, or a binding commitment, changed hands.

Rung eight is repeat purchase, retained usage, renewal, or margin improvement. The buyer came back, or stayed, or paid more.

The skill uses rungs one through three to design tests. It uses rungs six through eight to justify build, hiring, automation, or scaling. A buyer who pays today is more reliable than a hundred who say they would pay someday. The skill never confuses rungs.

## A Sample Interaction

Imagine you bring the skill the following question. You say: I want to build a SaaS that helps freelance designers manage client feedback rounds. Designers waste hours chasing approvals. I'm thinking about building it. Should I?

A typical AI tool would generate a roadmap, suggest features, and offer encouragement. Outcome-First Decisions answers differently.

The verdict: test first. Do not build.

The reasoning: designers waste hours chasing approvals is a real-sounding problem, but the offer is unvalidated, the buyer is loosely defined — freelance designers is a category, not a segment — and there is no evidence any of them will pay to fix it. Building before a paid pilot is the most common failure path for solo SaaS. Months of code, no buyer, no channel.

The evidence read: the friction is real, but only at rung one or two — opinion plus your own observation. There is no named buyer, no money on the table, no channel that reaches this buyer repeatedly.

The proof test: a Cash Proof Sprint. Seven days. A 14-day approval-cycle audit — review the buyer's last five client projects, identify the cycles that lost time, give a one-page tightening plan. Two hundred fifty dollars, paid before. Direct outreach to twenty-five freelance brand designers billing five thousand dollars or more per project, with three or more active clients.

The kill criterion: zero paid audits after twenty-five contacts. The pain is not painful enough to pay for. Capture the learning. Stop.

The next three actions: write the twenty-five-name outreach list. Write the four-sentence outreach message ending with a paid-deposit ask and a Friday deadline. Send the first five messages today.

That is the skill in action. Verdict, evidence, proof test, kill criterion, three actions for today. Six parts. No more.

## What Makes It Different

Most AI tools soften when evidence is weak. Outcome-First Decisions does the opposite. When evidence is rung one or two, the verdict is "test first" — never "looks promising, here's a roadmap."

The skill tracks calibration. Every prediction is logged with a stated confidence level, in ten-percent increments. Once ten or more entries exist in the same category — validation, prioritization, pricing, hiring, partnership, offer, channel — the skill cites the user's actual hit rate inline. If you predicted eighty percent confidence on validation calls and hit forty-two percent of them, the skill will say so. It will discount your next eighty percent claim accordingly. The math is shown. The user sees their own track record.

The skill names blind spots. If a user habitually skips rung six before committing to build, the skill flags it inline on the next decision in that domain. Patterns are not labels. They are observations the agent surfaces when relevant.

The skill has a Crisis Mode. When the user mentions runway under ninety days, missed payroll, a lost biggest customer, or invokes slash crisis-mode, the skill compresses output to verdict, three hour-level actions, and the dollar threshold below which the business closes. Worth Filter scoring tables and Evidence Ladder discussion are skipped. In crisis, full strategy frameworks are themselves busywork.

## What's Inside the Package

The skill ships as a single downloadable package. Inside are six core frameworks: Worth Filter, Cash Proof Sprint, One Number Map, Kill List Audit, Buyer-Problem-Path, and Leverage Ladder. Plus seven supporting frameworks for narrower situations.

There are twenty-three named anti-patterns — behaviors the skill will call out by name when they appear, like Polishing Before Proof, Automation Theater, and Founder Bottleneck. Sixteen operating principles. Twenty-one mental models.

Five industry overlays: SaaS, services and agencies, creator economy, e-commerce, and B2B. Each overlay defines the unit of value for that vertical, the most-believed-but-wrong narrative, the rung-six buyer-behavior signals to track, and the proof test that's hardest to fake.

A buyer-conversation kit: cold-outreach templates that ask for behavior not opinion, a customer-interview guide that bans the phrase "would you," pre-sale ask language with refund policies, and a five-category objection-handling diagnostic.

A decision journal with a queryable YAML schema. Every keep, change, or kill verdict gets logged with a stated confidence, a deadline, a category, and a kill criterion. After the deadline passes, the outcome and the lesson get filled in. Drifted decisions — entries past their deadline with no recorded outcome — get auto-flagged as decision debt.

A blind-spots register that tracks the rungs you habitually skip and the anti-patterns that recur. The register is the agent's note, not a dashboard. The skill translates patterns into specific current-context language: "you typically commit to building before rung six, the current case is at rung three, make this a test."

Three nested subskills for narrow flows: validate-idea, kill-list, and offer-sharpener. Five Claude Code slash commands: slash validate, slash worth-filter, slash kill-audit, slash sharpen, and slash weekly-review.

A seven-day Stuck-to-Shipped workflow chain for the most common user state: I'm busy and nothing is moving. The chain runs Day Zero Kill List Audit, Days One through Two One Number Map, Days Three through Seven Cash Proof Sprint with daily check-ins, and an automatic Day Seven verdict.

## Who It's For

Outcome-First Decisions is built for people whose scarcest resource is decisive attention. Founders who need to validate before they build. Operators who need to cut before they hire. Creators who need to measure paying buyers, not subscribers. Consultants who need to productize before they scale. Teams who need one scoreboard number, three actions, and the discipline to log a kill criterion in writing.

It is not for people who want a strategy memo or a five-page pitch deck. It is not for people who need encouragement to start. It is for people who are already starting and want fewer wrong decisions per quarter.
