# Objection Handling

A five-stage objection map. Buyer objections cluster into five categories. Diagnose the category before responding.

Most objection-handling advice is a script. This is a diagnostic — the buyer is telling the user which part of the offer is weak. Listen for the category, then respond by sharpening that part of the offer, not by rebutting the buyer.

## The Five Categories

1. **Price.** "Too expensive" / "out of budget" / "let me check pricing options."
2. **Authority.** "I need to check with [boss / partner / team]" / "this isn't my call."
3. **Time.** "Not now" / "circle back next quarter" / "we're focused on something else."
4. **Fit.** "We do it differently" / "we tried X and it didn't work" / "I'm not sure this applies."
5. **Trust.** "How do I know this works?" / "do you have references?" / silent stalling.

## Diagnostic First, Response Second

Before responding to any objection, ask one clarifying question:

> "When you say [their objection], is it more about [category A] or [category B]?"

The buyer's clarification routes the response. A "too expensive" that turns out to be authority is a different conversation than a "too expensive" that is genuinely about budget.

## Category 1: Price

**Surface form:** "Too expensive."

**Real diagnostic question:**

- Is the price unclear? → sharpen the offer description.
- Is the value unclear? → quantify cost of inaction (see `customer-interview-guide.md`).
- Is the comparison unclear? → name what the buyer is comparing against and address that comparison directly.
- Is the ROI cycle unclear? → state the payback period in writing.

**Response patterns:**

- "Compared to what?" — Forces the buyer to surface the alternative being weighed.
- "What's the cost to your business if [problem] continues for another 12 months?" — Reframes the question against the status quo.
- "If I cut the price by 30%, would you sign today?" — A "yes" reveals price was the issue; a "no" reveals price was a stand-in for another category.

**Don't:** discount as the first response. Discount before pushback trains the buyer to wait for it on every renewal.

## Category 2: Authority

**Surface form:** "I need to check with [someone]."

**Real diagnostic question:**

- Who is the actual economic buyer?
- What does that person need to see to say yes?
- Has that person ever bought something similar? On what terms?

**Response patterns:**

- "Who else should be in the next conversation?" — Skips the proxy and pulls in the decider.
- "What does [decision maker] need to see by when?" — Surfaces their decision criteria.
- "I'll send a one-page summary you can forward to [decision maker]. What does it need to address for them to greenlight?" — Lets the proxy advocate, but delivers material to the decider directly.

**Don't:** assume the proxy will sell internally. They will not. Build the material that sells without you in the room.

## Category 3: Time

**Surface form:** "Not now."

**Real diagnostic question:**

- Is the problem not yet acute? → set a re-trigger condition.
- Is the buyer overcommitted? → the offer is not the bottleneck; their capacity is.
- Is timing a polite no? → confirm with one clarifying question.

**Response patterns:**

- "What would need to be true in [3 / 6 / 12] months for this to be a yes?" — Gets the re-trigger condition in writing.
- "If [re-trigger] happens, can I check back on [date]?" — Sets the next date.
- "Is 'not now' more like 'not yet' or more like 'never'? Both are useful answers." — Removes the politeness layer.

**Don't:** stay in pipeline forever on a "not now." Set a specific re-trigger; if the trigger doesn't happen by a stated date, kill the lead.

## Category 4: Fit

**Surface form:** "We do it differently" / "we tried X and it didn't work."

**Real diagnostic question:**

- Is the prior attempt comparable? Or are they comparing against something with very different inputs?
- Is the buyer different enough that the offer genuinely doesn't apply? Or is the user trying to fit a square peg?
- Is "we do it differently" a real method, or a reluctance to change?

**Response patterns:**

- "Tell me about [their approach] — what's working, what isn't?" — Surfaces real diagnosis vs. inertia.
- "What was different about [past attempt]?" — Distinguishes a comparable failure from an unrelated one.
- "If the offer doesn't fit, who do you know it would fit?" — Turns a no into a referral if the answer is genuine.

**Don't:** convince a buyer their method is wrong. Either the offer fits or it doesn't. Forcing fit produces churn six months later.

## Category 5: Trust

**Surface form:** "How do I know this works?" / silent stalling.

**Real diagnostic question:**

- Is credibility missing? → references, case studies, named outcomes.
- Is safety missing? → guarantee, refund, escape clause, success criteria in writing.
- Is the user asking the buyer to take an irrational amount of risk for the price? → reduce the risk through scope or guarantee.

**Response patterns:**

- "Who would you trust if they said this worked? Let me see if I can introduce you." — Trust comes from peers more than from the user.
- "If we don't hit [outcome] by [date], here's what happens [refund / extension / credit]." — Puts the user's stake on the table.
- "What's the smallest version we could try that would let you make a clean call?" — Reduces scope to make the risk match the trust level.

**Don't:** add more credibility content. The user has usually shipped enough; the buyer is asking for safety, not credentials.

## Output: After Each Objection

Track every objection in the outreach log:

```text
Buyer:           [name]
Surface objection: [their words]
Diagnosed category: [price / authority / time / fit / trust]
Response used:   [pattern]
Result:          [advanced / stalled / removed]
Next step:       [ask + date]
```

After 10 logged objections, look for patterns. The most-common diagnosed category is the part of the offer to sharpen first — not the part to write more rebuttals for.
